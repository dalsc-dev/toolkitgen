Understanding Display Pixels

A pixel (picture element) is the smallest unit of a digital image on a display. Each pixel consists of three subpixels—red, green, and blue—that combine to create millions of colors. When one or more of these subpixels fail, you get visible defects.

How LCD and OLED Displays Work

LCD (Liquid Crystal Display)

LCD screens use liquid crystals sandwiched between two conductive panels. The crystals block or allow light from a backlight, creating the image. Pixels are illuminated by a constant backlight.

OLED (Organic Light-Emitting Diode)

OLED screens have self-emissive pixels that produce their own light. Each pixel can be completely turned off, creating true blacks and vibrant colors.

Types of Pixel Defects

Dead Pixels

A dead pixel is completely non-functional—it doesn't receive power and appears as a tiny black dot regardless of what color should be displayed. Dead pixels are permanent defects that cannot be repaired.

Causes of Dead Pixels

  • Manufacturing defects: Subpixel failure during production
  • Physical damage: Impact or pressure on the screen
  • Age deterioration: Components wearing out over time
  • Heat damage: Excessive temperature affecting liquid crystals

Stuck Pixels

A stuck pixel is a subpixel that's permanently on, showing a bright dot of a single color (red, green, or blue) against the background. Unlike dead pixels, stuck pixels may sometimes be fixed.

Types of Stuck Pixels

  • Hot pixel: Always on at full brightness (white)
  • Stuck subpixel: One color channel stuck (red, green, or blue)
  • Transient stuck pixel: Only appears in certain conditions

Dead Subpixels

A dead subpixel affects only one color channel. If the red subpixel dies, the pixel can only show cyan (green+blue) or black. This creates subtle color shifts in certain areas.

Stuck Subpixels

A stuck subpixel is always on for one color. The affected pixel shows a slight tint of that color. For example, a stuck red subpixel causes a pinkish or reddish hue in that pixel.

Lazy Pixels

Lazy pixels respond slowly to color changes. They may appear to "lag" behind other pixels, especially when displaying rapid transitions. This is more common in older displays.

Dead Pixel Policies and Standards

ISO 13406-2 Standard

The ISO 13406-2 standard defines acceptable pixel defect levels for flat panel displays. It classifies defects into four types:

Type 1: Always-on pixel (bright dot)

A pixel that always outputs light regardless of image content. This is a stuck bright pixel.

Type 2: Always-off pixel (dark dot)

A pixel that never outputs light. This is a dead pixel.

Type 3: Stuck subpixels (bright or dark)

Individual R, G, or B subpixels that are permanently on or off, causing color shifts.

Type 4: Cluster defects

Multiple adjacent pixel defects in a localized area.

Manufacturer Policies

Zero Dead Pixel Policies

Some manufacturers offer "zero dead pixel" guarantees, replacing any display with even a single defect.

Tiered Policies

Many manufacturers allow a small number of defects depending on pixel zone:

  • Zone A (center): Strictest limits (0-1 pixels)
  • Zone B (edges): More lenient limits

Standard Allowances

Common acceptable defect thresholds:

  • 2-5 bright pixels
  • 2-5 dark pixels
  • 0-15 total stuck subpixels

Trying to Fix Stuck Pixels

While dead pixels cannot be repaired, stuck pixels sometimes can be fixed with these methods:

Method 1: Wait and Restart

Sometimes stuck pixels resolve themselves after a few hours. Try leaving the display off overnight and restarting.

Method 2: JScreenFix

JScreenFix and similar tools rapidly cycle colors on a small area, sometimes "unsticking" stuck pixels through rapid subpixel cycling.

Method 3: Pressure Method

Turn off the display, apply gentle pressure with a soft cloth directly over the stuck pixel for 30-60 seconds, then restart. This can redistribute liquid crystals.

Method 4: Heat Method

A warm (not hot) cloth applied to the area may help. Heat can sometimes reactivate stuck liquid crystals.

Method 5: Running Colors

Display rapidly changing colors across the affected area for extended periods (15-30 minutes). Some users report success with this method.

Prevention and Care

Proper Handling

  • Avoid pressing on LCD screens
  • Handle devices carefully during transport
  • Use screen protectors when appropriate

Environmental Factors

  • Avoid extreme temperatures
  • Keep displays away from moisture
  • Use surge protectors to prevent electrical damage

Software Considerations

  • Use screensavers or power management to prevent burn-in on OLED
  • Update graphics drivers regularly
  • Avoid static images for extended periods on OLED

When to Seek Warranty Service

Within Warranty Period

If your display is still under warranty and shows dead pixels beyond manufacturer tolerances, contact the manufacturer or retailer for replacement.

Document Everything

  • Screenshots or photos if possible

Testing Different Display Types

Smartphone Screens

Smartphone OLED screens are particularly susceptible to burn-in and dead pixels. Test regularly and use dark modes to extend panel life.

Laptop Displays

Laptop screens can develop dead pixels from pressure (closing with objects on keyboard) or physical damage. Test new laptops immediately.

Gaming Monitors

Gaming monitors with high refresh rates and fast response times demand more from pixels. Test thoroughly for both dead pixels and image quality.

TV Screens

Large TV displays make pixel defects more visible. Check in both bright and dark viewing conditions.

Tablet and E-Reader Displays

Test all-in-one tablets for dead pixels. E-ink displays have different failure modes than LCD/OLED.

Advanced Pixel Testing Patterns

Single Color Screens

Display pure R, G, B, White, and Black. Any deviation indicates a pixel problem.

Inversion Tests

Rapidly alternate between two contrasting colors to detect transient stuck pixels.

Gradient Tests

Smooth color gradients can reveal lazy pixels that respond slowly.

Noise Patterns

Random noise can sometimes "wake up" stuck pixels and reveal defects that only appear under specific conditions.

Understanding Subpixel Layouts

R-Stripe (RGB)

Subpixels arranged vertically with Red, Green, Blue in sequence. Common in many LCD panels.

B-Stripe (BGR)

Reversed arrangement of BGR subpixels. Often found in TN panels.

Stripe (RGB/RGB)

The most common layout, with repeating RGB triplets.

PenTile (RG-BG)

Uses fewer green subpixels, with twice as many red and blue. Common in AMOLED displays.

Diamond Pixel

Samsung's Diamond Pentile layout with diamond-shaped subpixels.

Frequently Asked Questions About Dead Pixels

1. What is a dead pixel?

A dead pixel is a pixel that doesn't receive power and appears as a permanent black dot regardless of what color should be displayed.

2. What is the difference between dead and stuck pixels?

A dead pixel is completely non-functional (black). A stuck pixel is permanently on, showing a bright dot of one color (red, green, or blue).

3. Can dead pixels be fixed?

No, dead pixels are permanent hardware failures. Stuck pixels sometimes can be fixed using pixel fixing software or pressure methods.

4. How do I test my screen for dead pixels?

Use our Dead Pixel Checker to display solid colors. Look for any dots that don't match the screen color, especially on red, green, and blue tests.

5. How many dead pixels are acceptable?

This varies by manufacturer. Some allow 2-5 dead pixels, while premium displays often have zero tolerance. Check your warranty.

6. Can stuck pixels become dead pixels?

Yes, stuck pixels can sometimes progress to dead pixels over time. Try fixing them early while they're still stuck.

7. How long does it take to find a dead pixel?

Using our checker, you can thoroughly test your screen in 2-3 minutes by cycling through all test colors.

8. Can pressure fix stuck pixels?

Sometimes. The pressure method (gentle pressure with soft cloth) can redistribute liquid crystals and resolve stuck pixels.

9. Does JScreenFix actually work?

JScreenFix has mixed results. It works best on recently stuck pixels and may require multiple sessions.

10. What causes dead pixels?

Dead pixels are caused by manufacturing defects, physical damage, heat damage, or component aging.

11. Can dead pixels spread?

Physical dead pixels don't typically spread. However, physical damage might affect neighboring pixels.

12. Are OLED pixels more prone to failure?

OLED pixels can suffer from burn-in (gradual degradation) and individual pixel death, though manufacturing quality has improved.

13. Should I buy a screen with dead pixels?

Only if heavily discounted and within manufacturer warranty. Otherwise, return it and get a replacement.

14. How do I prevent dead pixels?

Avoid physical damage, extreme temperatures, and pressure on screens. Use surge protectors and handle devices carefully.

15. What's the warranty for dead pixels on new monitors?

Warranties vary by manufacturer. Some have zero dead pixel policies; others allow 2-5 defects depending on screen zone.

16. Can software updates fix dead pixels?

No, dead pixels are hardware failures that cannot be repaired with software.

17. How do I test my phone for dead pixels?

Use our Dead Pixel Checker in fullscreen mode on your phone. Test all primary colors and look for any spots that don't match.

18. What is a subpixel?

A subpixel is one of the three color components (red, green, or blue) that make up each pixel. Dead subpixels cause color shifts.

19. Can dead pixels appear later?

Yes, dead pixels can appear over time due to aging, heat damage, or physical trauma. They're not always present from new.

20. Is the ISO 13406-2 standard still relevant?

It's been superseded by ISO 9241-307, but the general principles of pixel defect classification are still widely referenced.

21. How do I return a monitor with dead pixels?

Contact the retailer or manufacturer within the return window. Document the defects with photos and screenshots from our checker.

22. Can E-ink displays have dead pixels?

E-ink displays can have stuck or dead "pixels" (actually microcapsules), visible as white or black spots.

23. What about dead pixels on curved monitors?

Curved monitors have the same pixel defect risks as flat panels. Testing is especially important as defects are more visible.

24. Can gaming cause dead pixels?

Gaming itself doesn't cause dead pixels, but leaving static HUD elements on OLED screens can cause burn-in, not dead pixels.

Our checker displays accurate pure colors across the entire screen, making even subtle pixel defects clearly visible.
