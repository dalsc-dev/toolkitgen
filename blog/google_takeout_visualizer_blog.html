Route tracking captures the paths you take between places, including mode of transport. Walking routes, driving trips, flights, and public transit all get logged. Each route includes start and end points, the path taken, and estimated time of travel.

Activity inference uses sensors and algorithms to determine how you were traveling. Walking, running, cycling, driving, and sitting in vehicles are all recognized based on movement patterns. This activity data provides additional context to your location records.

Place clustering groups visits to similar locations over time, identifying places that appear to be your home, workplace, gym, or regular destinations. This aggregation provides a profile of your routine and habits.

How to Download Your Google Location Data

Before visualizing your data, you need to download it through Google Takeout. This process creates export files containing your complete location history in structured JSON format. Understanding the download process ensures you get the most complete data for visualization.

Step 1: Access Google Takeout. Visit takeout.google.com and sign in with the Google account whose data you want to export. If you have multiple accounts, you'll need to export from each one separately.

Step 2: Select your data. Click "Deselect all" and then specifically check "Location History." You may also want "Semantic Location History" for more detailed records. Only selecting what's needed speeds up the export process and reduces file size.

Step 3: Configure export options. Choose your file type (JSON is best for our visualizer), frequency (one-time export is fine), and destination. The export will be emailed to you when ready if you choose cloud delivery, or you can download directly.

Step 4: Receive and extract. Depending on data volume, the export may take minutes to hours. You'll receive an email when ready. Download the ZIP file and extract it to access the JSON files containing your location data.

Once you have your JSON files, our Map Data Visualizer can process them to create your personal movement map. The tool works directly in your browser, so your data remains on your device throughout the process.

The Technology Behind Location Tracking

Understanding how Google determines your location helps explain the accuracy—and limitations—of the data. Multiple technologies combine to create increasingly precise location records.

GPS tracking uses signals from satellites to determine precise location, typically accurate to within a few meters outdoors. Most smartphones have GPS chips that constantly record location when enabled. However, GPS doesn't work well indoors and can be blocked by buildings, weather, or dense tree cover.

Wi-Fi positioning determines location by detecting nearby Wi-Fi networks and comparing them against databases of known network locations. Even without connecting to Wi-Fi, your phone can detect networks and use them for positioning. This works well indoors and in urban areas with many networks.

Cell tower triangulation uses mobile network base stations to estimate location. Less precise than GPS or Wi-Fi, cell positioning provides rough location even when other methods aren't available. Your approximate location is known as long as your phone has any cellular connection.

Sensor fusion combines data from accelerometers, gyroscopes, and barometers with the above methods to improve accuracy and determine activity. Movement patterns help verify whether you're walking, driving, or stationary, and can fill gaps when other signals are weak.

Inertial navigation estimates movement based on acceleration and rotation sensors, allowing approximate tracking between known location points. This is why your location continues to be recorded even in tunnels or areas with poor GPS signal.

Data Accuracy and Limitations

When visualizing your data, you'll notice some entries seem incorrect—points in impossible locations, jumps in position that seem to defy physics, or gaps where you expected data. Understanding these limitations helps interpret your location history accurately.

GPS drift causes points to appear slightly off from actual location. In areas with poor satellite visibility (urban canyons, indoors), GPS accuracy degrades significantly. A point appearing in the middle of a building across the street from where you actually stood is common GPS behavior.

Jump artifacts appear when positioning methods change. A sudden jump from one location to another across town in seconds indicates a GPS correction, not actual movement. These artifacts should be ignored when analyzing your data.

Missing data is common when devices lose power, go into airplane mode, or have location services disabled. You may have been somewhere significant but simply didn't have location recording active.

Stale locations occur when the system hasn't updated position recently. Points might show you at an old location even if you've actually moved. The timestamp indicates when the data was recorded, but sometimes there's confusion about recency.

Google location settings control what data is collected across Google's services. These can be found in your Google Account settings and include Location History, Activity controls, and personalization options.

The Future of Location Tracking

Location tracking technology continues to evolve, becoming more precise and more integrated into daily life. Understanding emerging trends helps you prepare for future privacy challenges.

BLE beacons are appearing in retail environments, providing extremely precise indoor positioning. These create location records at the level of specific product aisles, enabling detailed behavior tracking in physical spaces.

Fingerprinting advances allow location estimation even without GPS or network positioning. Device characteristics, ambient signals, and environmental features can indicate location with increasing accuracy.

IoT integration means more devices track location. Cars, wearables, and smart home devices all collect and transmit location data. The scope of location tracking is expanding beyond phones.

Regulatory changes continue to evolve. GDPR, CCPA, and emerging regulations in other jurisdictions provide some rights regarding location data. However, enforcement varies and regulation may not keep pace with technology.
