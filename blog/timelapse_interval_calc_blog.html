Understanding Timelapse Photography Basics

Timelapse is a cinematography technique that compresses time by capturing frames at much slower rates than they'll be played back. A scene unfolding over hours or days becomes seconds of video, revealing movements too slow to perceive in real-time. Clouds drift dramatically, flowers bloom in seconds, and city streets pulse with activity that seems impossibly fast.

The mathematics are straightforward: if you capture one frame every 5 seconds for one hour (3,600 seconds), you capture 720 frames. Playing these back at 24 frames per second creates a 30-second video representing one hour of real-time. The interval between shots determines how much time compression occurs.

The artistic challenge lies in choosing intervals that create the intended effect. Too fast, and the timelapse becomes chaotic. Too slow, and motion appears unnatural. The right interval tells the story you want to tell about the subject's movement through time.

How Interval Affects Motion Smoothness

Perceptual smoothness at typical playback rates (24-60fps) requires sufficient frame capture to create fluid motion. Each playback second shows 24-60 frames from the timelapse sequence. Faster action and more dramatic time compression require shorter intervals for smooth results.

Motion blur in timelapse comes from two sources: the exposure time of each frame and the movement occurring during that exposure. Fast-moving subjects like traffic need short exposures to freeze motion. Slower subjects like clouds can tolerate longer exposures without visible blur.

Flicker reduction benefits from consistent exposure settings. Any variation in brightness between frames creates visible flicker in the final video. Manual exposure settings, stable lighting, and consistent intervals all contribute to smooth results.

Movement trails occur when exposure times are long relative to movement speed. Car headlights leave trails, people become ghosts, and clouds smear across frames. This can be intentional artistic effect or an unwanted artifact depending on your goals.

Subject-Specific Interval Recommendations

Clouds and weather typically require 3-10 second intervals. Fast-moving storm clouds need shorter intervals (2-5 seconds) to capture their movement smoothly. Slow, dramatic cloudscapes can use longer intervals (5-15 seconds) for a dreamy effect.

City traffic and urban activity works well with 1-5 second intervals. Fast traffic on highways needs shorter intervals; pedestrian-level city scenes with walking people can use longer intervals. Experiment to find the balance between smooth motion and dramatic speed.

Sun movement and shadows requires careful calculation based on latitude and time of day. The sun moves 15 degrees per hour—slower than most think. Intervals of 30-120 seconds often work well, with shorter intervals for sunset/sunrise sequences.

Stars and night sky presents special challenges. The stars move 15 degrees per hour (360 degrees per 24 hours), so star trails or smooth star movement requires 15-45 second intervals. Use the 500 rule (500 divided by focal length) to determine maximum exposure before star trails appear.

Construction and growth over days or weeks requires longer intervals from minutes to hours. Construction site timelapses might capture one frame every 5-30 minutes, producing dramatic progress videos showing buildings rising from foundations.

Playback Frame Rate Considerations

24fps (cinematic) provides the classic film look with natural motion perception. Most feature films use this frame rate, creating familiar viewing experiences. 24fps timelapses appear dramatic and deliberate.

30fps is the standard for broadcast video in North America and Japan. It provides smooth motion that's familiar to television viewers. Many consumer cameras default to 30fps for video recording.

60fps produces ultra-smooth motion suitable for high-quality productions and slow-motion extraction. 60fps timelapses can be slowed to 50% speed while remaining smooth, providing editing flexibility.

Time-stretching in post-production allows converting 24fps footage to 30fps by holding frames, or vice versa. However, maintaining the original frame rate throughout production simplifies workflow and preserves quality.

Calculating Total Shooting Duration

Scene duration math works in reverse from video duration. To get 30 seconds of 24fps video, you need 720 frames (30 × 24). At a 5-second interval, this requires 3,600 seconds or 1 hour of shooting time.

Available light windows constrain outdoor timelapses. Golden hour lasts 1-2 hours, full daylight transitions span 3-4 hours, and blue hour is only 20-40 minutes. Plan intervals to capture the entire desired light window.

Storage capacity planning ensures you have enough memory for your shoot. RAW files at 25MB each × 1,000 frames = 25GB. JPEG files at 5MB each × 1,000 frames = 5GB. Plan card capacity accordingly.

Battery life planning is essential for long timelapses. Calculate total frames needed, multiply by exposure time and interval overhead, and ensure you have enough battery capacity for the entire shoot plus buffer.

Interval Shooting Modes and Features

Built-in intervalometers in many cameras allow setting intervals and total frames without external triggers. Program these before your shoot and let the camera run automatically.

External intervalometers provide more control and reliability for critical shoots. They connect to the camera's shutter release port and offer programmable sequences, bulb ramping, and exposure smoothing features.

Magic Lantern and CHDK firmware add interval shooting to Canon and some other cameras. These free alternatives provide professional features without purchasing external hardware.

Smartphone apps for timelapse often include interval calculation, remote control, and cloud connection for monitoring shoots remotely. They work well for casual timelapse but may lack precision for critical work.

Exposure Considerations for Timelapse

Flicker prevention through exposure consistency is crucial. Automatic exposure modes cause brightness variations as light changes, creating visible frame-to-frame flicker. Manual exposure or exposure smoothing settings prevent this.

Ramping adjusts exposure gradually during the shoot to accommodate changing light. Sunrise/sunset timelapses need longer exposures at the beginning and shorter exposures at the end. Advanced intervalometers handle ramping automatically.

Exposure to interval ratio matters when shooting fast action. If your exposure is 1/250 second but your interval is 3 seconds, the camera is idle for 99% of the interval. Fast action can use shorter intervals without wasting much time.

Silent shooting modes reduce vibration and noise during timelapse capture, important for stationary subjects where camera movement between frames would be visible.

Storage and Workflow Planning

RAW vs. JPEG choice affects storage dramatically. RAW files capture maximum quality but are 5-10x larger than JPEGs. For timelapse, evaluate whether post-production flexibility of RAW justifies storage and workflow complexity.

Card capacity calculation determines how many frames you can capture before changing cards. A 128GB card holding JPEG files at 10MB each stores 12,800 frames—enough for over 10 hours at 5-second intervals.

Backup strategy for important shoots should include in-camera backup to multiple cards, tethering to a laptop, or cloud upload during shoots. Losing hours of timelapse footage to card failure is devastating.

Post-production workflow for timelapse involves importing thousands of images, batch processing, assembling into video, and color grading. Plan computing resources for the scale of your timelapse.

Special Timelapse Techniques

Holy Grail timelapse captures sunrise or sunset with dramatic exposure changes. This technique requires careful exposure ramping to maintain detail through the brightest and darkest moments without flicker.

Hyperlapse adds camera movement between frames, creating smooth timelapse sequences with dramatic perspective changes. This advanced technique requires stabilization and careful planning for movement paths.

Night timelapse requires longer exposures, higher ISO settings, and careful attention to focus and noise. Star trails, light trails, and illuminated cityscapes are popular night timelapse subjects.

Asteroid lens or 360-degree timelapse captures circular panoramas that show 360-degree views as time progresses. This unique perspective shows activity around a complete horizon in a circular frame.

Common Timelapse Mistakes to Avoid

Wrong interval choice is the most common mistake. Test your interval before the actual shoot to see how motion appears. What's theoretically correct may not look right in practice.

Insufficient battery or storage stops shoots prematurely. Calculate requirements conservatively and bring significantly more than you think you'll need.

Changing compositions from wind, vibrations, or accidental bumps make final sequences jerky or unusable. Use sturdy tripods and protect equipment from wind.

Focus drift with autofocus cameras causes soft frames when focus shifts slightly between shots. Lock focus manually and disable any autofocus features during timelapse capture.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What interval should I use for cloud timelapses?

Fast-moving storm clouds: 2-5 seconds. Slow, dramatic clouds: 5-15 seconds. Adjust based on how smooth you want the motion.

2. How do I calculate timelapse intervals?

Divide shooting duration (in seconds) by desired video duration (in seconds) multiplied by playback frame rate for the interval in seconds.

3. What frame rate should I use for timelapse?

24fps for cinematic, 30fps for broadcast standard, 60fps for ultra-smooth or slow-motion extraction. 24fps is most common for timelapse.

4. How long should I shoot for a 30-second timelapse?

At 24fps, 30 seconds needs 720 frames. At 5-second intervals, this requires 3,600 seconds or 1 hour of shooting.

5. Can I shoot timelapse with a smartphone?

Yes, most smartphones have built-in timelapse modes with interval calculation built in. Dedicated apps offer more control.

6. What's the best interval for sunset?

Golden hour (1-2 hours): 5-15 second intervals. Blue hour (20-40 minutes): 3-8 second intervals. Use exposure ramping for best results.

7. How do I prevent timelapse flicker?

Use manual or aperture-priority exposure with exposure smoothing. Avoid auto exposure modes that change between frames.

8. What interval for star timelapse?

15-45 seconds depending on focal length and desired effect. Shorter intervals for smooth star trails, longer for pinpoint stars.

9. How much storage for a 1-hour timelapse?

At 5-second intervals: 720 frames. At RAW (25MB): 18GB. At JPEG (8MB): 5.7GB.

10. Can I change intervals during a timelapse?

It's possible but creates inconsistent motion in the final video. Variable intervals are only used intentionally for special effects.

11. What's the 500 rule for night timelapse?

500 divided by focal length equals maximum exposure before star trails appear. At 24mm: 500/24 = ~20 seconds maximum.

12. How long can a camera shoot timelapse?

Limited only by storage and battery. Some cameras have built-in limits; external intervalometers remove these restrictions.

13. Should I use RAW or JPEG for timelapse?

RAW for maximum post-production flexibility. JPEG for smaller files and simpler workflow when quality is sufficient.

14. What is holy grail timelapse?

Timelapse spanning sunrise or sunset with dramatic light changes. Requires exposure ramping to maintain consistent brightness.

15. Can wind affect timelapse?

Yes, wind can move tripods and cause jerky or unusable frames. Use sturdy tripods and shield equipment from wind.

16. How do I calculate total timelapse frames?

Video duration (seconds) × playback frame rate = total frames needed.

17. What's bulb ramping?

Gradually adjusting exposure during long timelapses to accommodate changing light, typically for sunrise/sunset sequences.

18. Can I shoot timelapse in cold weather?

Yes, but batteries drain faster and some cameras have temperature limits. Bring extra batteries and keep them warm.

19. What's the best interval for traffic?

Light traffic on streets: 2-5 seconds. Highway traffic: 1-3 seconds. Adjust based on desired smoothness.

20. How do I focus for timelapse?

Use manual focus, autofocus once on your subject, then disable autofocus to prevent drift between frames.

21. What is hyperlapse?

Timelapse with camera movement between frames, creating smooth, dramatic sequences with perspective changes.

22. Can I extract stills from timelapse?

Yes, each frame is a complete photograph. Timelapse sequences are excellent sources for selecting the best single images.
