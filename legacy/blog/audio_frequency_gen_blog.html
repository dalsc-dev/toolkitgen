Understanding Audio Frequency Generation

Sound is vibration traveling through a medium (typically air) as a pressure wave. The frequency of this wave—measured in Hertz (Hz)—determines the pitch we perceive. One Hz equals one cycle per second, so a 440 Hz tone completes 440 pressure oscillations every second, which human ears perceive as the musical note A above middle C.

Understanding frequencies is fundamental to audio work. The human hearing range spans roughly 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz (20 kHz), though this range decreases with age and exposure to loud sounds. Understanding Frequency Ranges

Low Frequencies (20 Hz - 250 Hz)

Low frequencies include bass guitar, kick drums, organ notes, and room-shaking bass. Frequencies below 60 Hz are felt as much as heard—the tactile sensation of bass you feel in your chest. Testing low frequencies reveals how speakers and rooms handle bass reproduction.

Many small speakers and headphones cannot reproduce very low frequencies, so testing below 80 Hz often reveals frequency response limitations.

Midrange Frequencies (250 Hz - 4 kHz)

Midrange frequencies contain most musical information including vocals, guitars, horns, and most instruments. This range is crucial for clarity and intelligibility. Testing here reveals how equipment handles the most important part of the audio spectrum.

Room acoustics problems often manifest in the midrange, where reflections and standing waves can color the sound significantly.

High Frequencies (4 kHz - 20 kHz)

High frequencies provide air, sparkle, and detail in audio reproduction. Cymbals, high strings, breathy vocals, and delicate overtones live here. Testing high frequencies reveals tweeter performance and overall system detail capability.

Human hearing sensitivity to high frequencies decreases with age, typically becoming noticeable above 40 years old. Many adults cannot hear above 15-16 kHz.

Scientific Principles Behind Tone Generation

The Nature of Sound Waves

Sound travels as a longitudinal wave—molecules compress and rarefy in the direction of travel. A pure tone consists of sinusoidal pressure variations, where pressure smoothly oscillates above and below ambient atmospheric pressure.

The mathematical representation of a sine wave (y = sin(x)) perfectly describes the pressure variations of a pure tone. This mathematical purity is why sine waves are the preferred waveform for scientific and testing applications.

Frequency vs Pitch

Frequency is an objective, measurable property (cycles per second). Pitch is the subjective perception of frequency. While closely related, pitch perception can be affected by volume, harmonic content, and individual hearing characteristics.

Musical intervals correspond to specific frequency ratios: an octave represents a doubling of frequency, a perfect fifth is a 3:2 ratio, etc. Understanding these relationships is fundamental to music and audio work.

Hertz and Audio Measurement

The Hertz (Hz) is the SI unit of frequency, named after Heinrich Hertz who demonstrated radio waves in 1887. One Hz equals one cycle per second. Audio frequencies range from about 20 Hz (lowest audible bass) to about 20,000 Hz (highest audible treble).

Large ranges are often expressed in kilohertz (kHz): 1 kHz = 1000 Hz. The audible range extends to roughly 20 kHz, though this upper limit decreases with age.

Safety Considerations

Hearing Protection

Extended exposure to loud sounds can cause permanent hearing damage. When using a tone generator, especially at high amplitudes, protect your hearing. Start at low volumes and increase only as needed. Take breaks during extended testing sessions.

Sounds above 85 dB can cause hearing damage with sufficient exposure time. Be particularly cautious with frequencies in the 2-4 kHz range where the ear is most sensitive.

Equipment Protection

Before playing tones at significant volumes, ensure your speakers and equipment can handle the frequencies and levels you'll be generating. Very low frequencies can damage speakers not designed for deep bass reproduction, particularly small speakers or headphones.

Start at low volumes when testing any new frequencies or equipment. Gradually increase while monitoring for signs of distress like cone pumping, distortion, or unusual vibrations.

Practical Testing Procedures

Speaker Frequency Response Testing

To test speaker frequency response:

  • Start with a frequency sweep from 20 Hz to 20 kHz
  • Listen for frequencies that are louder or quieter than others
  • Note frequencies that sound distorted or strained
  • Test at multiple volume levels as response can vary with loudness
  • Compare multiple speakers to identify variations

Room Resonance Identification

To find room resonances:

  • Play frequency sweeps while walking around the room
  • Notice frequencies that seem louder or quieter at different positions
  • Identify "boomy" spots where bass seems exaggerated
  • Find "dead spots" where bass seems lacking
  • Use this information to guide speaker placement and acoustic treatment

Advanced Tone Generator Features

Custom Waveforms

While sine waves are fundamental, other waveforms have their uses. Square waves contain odd harmonics and are useful for testing digital circuits. Sawtooth waves are rich in harmonics and useful for synthesis and testing. Triangle waves have fewer harmonics than square waves.

The Online Tone Generator focuses on pure sine waves for maximum utility in testing and calibration, but understanding other waveforms expands your testing capabilities.

Modulation Options

Modulation—varying one aspect of the signal with another—opens additional testing possibilities. Amplitude modulation (AM) varies volume over time. Frequency modulation (FM) varies pitch over time. These can reveal different characteristics of audio equipment.

Some applications require modulated signals, and understanding modulation helps interpret testing results.
