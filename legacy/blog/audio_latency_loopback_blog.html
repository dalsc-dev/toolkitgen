What Is Audio Latency?

Audio latency is the time delay between when an audio signal enters a system and when it exits. In practical terms, it's the delay between playing a note on your MIDI controller and hearing the sound from your software instrument, or the gap between speaking into a microphone and hearing yourself in your headphones during recording.

Latency is measured in milliseconds (ms). One millisecond equals one-thousandth of a second. While this sounds tiny, human ears can detect delays as small as 10-15 ms, and for professional monitoring, latencies below 10 ms are ideal. Higher latencies can make real-time playing and recording impossible or extremely difficult.

Why Audio Latency Matters

Impact on Music Production

In music production, latency affects your ability to record and monitor in real-time. When recording vocals or instruments, you need to hear yourself with minimal delay to perform naturally. High latency causes timing issues, makes double-tracking nearly impossible, and creates an unnatural playing experience that affects your performance quality.

Software instruments also require low latency for responsive playing. If there's too much delay between pressing a key and hearing the sound, musical expression becomes difficult. Tight, rhythmic playing—essential for many genres—becomes nearly impossible with excessive latency.

Impact on Podcasting and Streaming

Podcasters and streamers encounter latency issues differently. When monitoring through software instead of hardware, the delay between speaking and hearing yourself can cause stuttering, awkward pauses, and difficulty maintaining conversational flow.

Live streaming with real-time effects (noise gates, compressors, EQ) requires sufficiently low latency that processing happens faster than real-time, otherwise effects lag behind the source audio.

Impact on Gaming and VR

In gaming and VR applications, audio latency affects immersion and competitive performance. Audio should synchronize perfectly with visual input. Late audio—sounds arriving after their source event—breaks immersion in games and can cause disorientation or nausea in VR.

Competitive gamers often prioritize low latency audio to gain advantages in games where sound cues are critical for gameplay.

Understanding the Audio Signal Chain

Input Latency

Input latency is the delay between the physical sound hitting your microphone (or signal entering your interface) and that signal being digitized and available to your computer. This includes the analog-to-digital conversion time and any preamplifier processing delay.

Quality audio interfaces minimize input latency through efficient converters and low-latency preamps. Understanding your input latency helps you account for it when timing-sensitive recording.

Processing Latency

Processing latency occurs within your computer as audio is routed through drivers, DAW software, and any plugins or virtual instruments. This is often the largest component of total system latency.

Your audio buffer size directly affects processing latency. Smaller buffers mean less latency but require more processing power. Larger buffers reduce CPU load but increase latency. Finding the right balance is crucial.

Output Latency

Output latency is the delay between your computer sending audio data and that signal emerging from your speakers or headphones. This includes digital-to-analog conversion time and any amplification delay.

Like input latency, output latency depends on your audio interface's quality. High-quality interfaces minimize output conversion time.

Total Round-Trip Latency

Total round-trip latency combines all three components: input latency + processing latency + output latency. This is what the Audio Latency Test measures when you route audio through your complete system.

For real-time monitoring, you care about the path from input through processing to output—the full round trip. Total latency below 10-15 ms is excellent for professional monitoring.

How Audio Interface Buffer Size Affects Latency

What Is Buffer Size?

Your audio buffer is a small amount of memory that holds audio data while it waits to be processed. Buffer size determines how much audio is processed at once. Smaller buffers = less latency but more CPU work. Larger buffers = more latency but easier on CPU.

Common buffer sizes range from 32 samples (very low latency) to 2048 samples or higher (higher latency). The optimal size depends on your CPU power, interface quality, and latency requirements.

Finding the Right Buffer Size

The goal is the smallest buffer size that your system can handle without audio dropouts or glitches. Start with a moderate buffer (512 samples) and gradually reduce it while monitoring performance. When you start hearing crackles, pops, or dropouts, you've found your minimum stable buffer size.

Typical results:

  • 64-128 samples: Very low latency (2-5 ms), requires powerful CPU
  • 256-512 samples: Good balance of latency and stability (5-10 ms)
  • 1024+ samples: Higher latency, safer for complex sessions

Factors Affecting Audio Latency

Audio Interface Quality

Higher-quality audio interfaces generally offer lower latency through better converter designs, more efficient drivers, and more stable clock circuits. Professional interfaces often specify latency at specific buffer sizes, but actual measurements vary based on system configuration.

USB, Thunderbolt, and PCIe interfaces each have different latency characteristics. Thunderbolt typically offers the lowest latency, while USB is common and adequate for most purposes.

Audio Driver Type

Your choice of audio driver significantly affects latency:

  • ASIO (Windows): Low-latency professional standard
  • Core Audio (macOS): Built-in, efficient, low latency
  • WASAPI (Windows): Competent but generally higher latency than ASIO
  • DirectSound (Windows): Older, generally higher latency

Always use the lowest-latency driver available for your platform. macOS Core Audio is generally excellent, while Windows users should prioritize ASIO drivers.

CPU Load and Performance

Your CPU must process audio fast enough to empty and fill buffers before new data is needed. High CPU usage from other applications or complex plugin chains increases effective latency, even with small buffer settings.

Close unnecessary applications during recording to free CPU resources. Consider freezing tracks or bouncing to reduce real-time processing load.

Sample Rate

Sample rate affects latency differently than you might expect. Higher sample rates (96 kHz, 192 kHz) actually require more frequent buffer processing, potentially increasing CPU load. However, they also provide better audio quality.

For latency-critical work, moderate sample rates (44.1 kHz or 48 kHz) often provide the best balance of quality and performance.

Reducing Audio Latency: Practical Solutions

Optimize Buffer Size

The most direct way to reduce latency is decreasing buffer size. However, there's a limit based on your CPU's ability to process audio in time. Use the Audio Latency Test to find your system's minimum stable buffer size.

For recording, use the smallest buffer your system handles without glitches. For mixing (where latency doesn't matter), larger buffers are fine and reduce CPU strain.

Use Direct Monitoring

Direct monitoring routes your input signal directly to output without going through software. This provides near-zero latency—you hear yourself in real-time while recording. Most interfaces and DAWs support this.

The trade-off is you won't hear software effects or virtual instruments in your monitoring. Many interfaces provide blend controls to mix direct and software-monitored signals.

Upgrade Your Interface

If latency remains problematic despite optimization, consider a higher-quality audio interface. Professional interfaces with Thunderbolt connectivity or dedicated DSP processing can achieve significantly lower latency than consumer USB interfaces.

Look for interfaces with low-latency specifications and efficient driver designs when latency is critical to your work.

Optimize Your System

Several system-level optimizations can help:

  • Use dedicated audio drive: Install your DAW on an SSD for faster access
  • Disable power management: Prevent CPU throttling during audio work
  • Update drivers: Interface and chipset drivers can affect performance
  • Disable unnecessary services: Reduce background processing load
  • Use performance power plan: Ensure CPU runs at full speed

Latency Requirements for Different Applications

Real-Time Recording and Performance

For recording live instruments and performing with virtual instruments, aim for total latency below 10 ms. Below 5 ms is ideal but requires optimized systems. Professional studios often achieve 3-5 ms with high-end equipment.

If you experience timing difficulties during recording, latency is likely too high. Test and optimize until playing feels natural.

Podcasting and Voice Recording

For podcasting, latency is less critical since you're not monitoring in real-time in most cases. However, if you use software monitoring with effects, keep latency below 20-30 ms to avoid discomfort when hearing yourself.

Many podcasters record with direct monitoring to avoid latency issues entirely.

Mixing and Mastering

Mixing and mastering don't require low latency since you're working with recorded material, not performing in real-time. Larger buffers are acceptable and reduce CPU load during these phases.

Switch buffer sizes based on your workflow phase—small for recording, larger for mixing.

Troubleshooting Common Latency Issues

Audio Dropouts and Glitches

If you experience audio dropouts, crackles, or pops:

  • Increase buffer size until stable
  • Reduce plugin count or freeze tracks
  • Close CPU-intensive applications
  • Update audio drivers
  • Check for hardware conflicts

High Latency Despite Small Buffers

If latency remains high even with small buffers:

  • Verify you're using the correct driver (ASIO/Core Audio)
  • Check interface is set as default device
  • Update interface firmware and drivers
  • Consider system-level latency issues
  • Test with only essential plugins enabled

Understanding Measurement Accuracy

Factors Affecting Measurement Precision

Audio latency measurements should be accurate to within a few milliseconds. However, slight variations occur based on system load, timing resolution, and how measurements are calculated.

For consistent results, take multiple measurements and use the average. Compare measurements with the same system conditions (same buffer size, same applications running).

Why Measurements Might Differ

Different latency test methods may produce slightly different results:

  • Loopback tests measure round-trip latency
  • Input-only tests measure half the latency
  • DAW sync tests measure apparent latency including compensation

The Audio Latency Test measures full round-trip latency, which is the relevant measurement for monitoring applications.

Advanced Latency Topics

Plugin Latency Compensation

Plugins introduce latency that can affect recording timing. Most DAWs automatically compensate for plugin latency, but some non-linear plugins (like some compressors or saturators) may not be fully compensated.

Understanding plugin latency helps you interpret measurements and configure your system correctly.

Hardware DSP Processing

Some high-end audio interfaces include onboard DSP processors for real-time effects without computer processing. These systems can achieve extremely low latency for monitoring with effects.

Hardware DSP solutions are used in professional live sound and high-end recording setups.
