Understanding Bluetooth Low Energy Technology

Bluetooth Low Energy, introduced as part of the Bluetooth 4.0 specification in 2010, revolutionized short-range wireless communication. Unlike classic Bluetooth, which was designed for continuous data streaming, BLE was optimized for intermittent data transmission with minimal power consumption. This efficiency opened entirely new categories of devices that could operate for months or years on small batteries.

BLE operates in the 2.4 GHz ISM band, the same frequency used by Wi-Fi, but employs a different modulation scheme and protocol stack. The technology supports data rates up to 2 Mbps (in Bluetooth 5.0 and later) while consuming a fraction of the power required by classic Bluetooth, making it ideal for IoT devices, wearables, and battery-powered sensors.

How BLE Scanning Works

The Discovery Process

When a BLE device is advertising, it periodically transmits packets containing information about its presence. These advertising packets include the device's unique address, available services, manufacturer data, and signal strength. A BLE scanner listens for these packets, collecting information from all discoverable devices in range.

The scanning process involves your device's Bluetooth radio switching between receiving advertising packets and performing other operations. This time-sharing allows scanning without completely blocking other Bluetooth activities, though aggressive scanning can impact other BLE operations.

Advertising Packets and Their Contents

BLE advertising packets contain several important fields:

  • Device Address: A unique 48-bit identifier for the device. May be public (burned into hardware) or random ( regenerated periodically for privacy).
  • Advertising Interval: How frequently the device transmits advertising packets, ranging from 20ms to 10,240ms.
  • Service UUIDs: Identifiers for services the device offers, allowing scanners to identify device types and capabilities.
  • Service Data: Additional data associated with advertised services.
  • Manufacturer Data: Data specific to the device manufacturer, often including device type or model information.
  • Tx Power Level: The device's transmitted power, used for proximity estimation.

The Bluetooth LE Scanner displays all this information, helping you understand what each discovered device is broadcasting.

Signal Strength and Proximity

Received Signal Strength Indicator (RSSI) measures the signal strength of advertising packets, measured in decibels relative to a milliwatt (dBm). Typical BLE RSSI values range from -40 dBm (very close) to -100 dBm (at the edge of range). By comparing a device's advertised transmit power with received signal strength, you can estimate proximity.

RSSI alone isn't sufficient for precise distance estimation because radio signals are affected by reflections, absorption, and interference. However, tracking RSSI changes over time can indicate whether a device is moving closer or farther away.

Technical Deep Dive: BLE Protocol

The BLE Protocol Stack

The BLE protocol stack consists of several layers:

  • Physical Layer: The 2.4 GHz radio and baseband modulation.
  • Link Layer: Manages advertising, scanning, and connections.
  • Host Controller Interface (HCI): Standardized communication between host and controller.
  • Logical Link Control and Adaptation Protocol (L2CAP): Multiplexes data between protocols.
  • Attribute Protocol (ATT): Defines how data is organized and accessed.
  • Security Manager (SM): Handles pairing and encryption.
  • Generic Access Profile (GAP): Defines device roles and advertising.
  • Generic Attribute Profile (GATT): Defines how data is exchanged via services and characteristics.

The Bluetooth LE Scanner primarily observes GAP advertising, but can connect to devices to explore GATT services if the device accepts connections.

Service UUIDs and Characteristics

GATT services organize related data into collections of characteristics. Services are identified by UUIDs (Universally Unique Identifiers), with standardized services defined by Bluetooth SIG and custom services using 16-bit or 128-bit UUIDs.

Common standardized services include:

  • Heart Rate Service: 0x180D - Contains heart rate measurement and control point characteristics.
  • Battery Service: 0x180F - Reports battery level.
  • Device Information Service: 0x180A - Contains manufacturer, model, and serial number information.
  • Current Time Service: 0x1805 - Provides current time.

The scanner shows advertised service UUIDs, and connecting to devices reveals full service trees with readable and writable characteristics.

BLE Addressing and Privacy

BLE devices use 48-bit MAC addresses similar to other networking technologies. Public addresses are assigned by manufacturers and never change, while random addresses may be regenerated periodically or for each connection.

The privacy feature in BLE allows devices to use resolvable private addresses that change frequently, preventing long-term tracking while still allowing authenticated devices to recognize each other. This privacy mechanism is important for personal devices but complicates security monitoring and device management.

Troubleshooting with the Bluetooth LE Scanner

Device Won't Connect

If a BLE device doesn't respond to connection attempts:

  • Verify advertising: Confirm the device appears in scan results.
  • Check distance: Move closer—some devices have limited range.
  • Remove existing bonds: Previously paired devices may need pairing cleared.
  • Restart the device: Power cycling can resolve temporary issues.
  • Check compatibility: Some devices only connect with specific apps or gateways.

The Bluetooth LE Scanner can verify that a device is advertising correctly, narrowing down connection issues to pairing, compatibility, or device-side problems.

Intermittent Connectivity

Intermittent BLE connections often result from:

  • Environmental interference: Wi-Fi, microwave ovens, or other BLE devices.
  • Insufficient advertising: Long advertising intervals make devices harder to find.
  • Power management: Devices reducing power to conserve battery.
  • Range limits: Approaching maximum operational distance.
  • Obstacles: Walls, metal objects, and human bodies attenuate signals.

Scanning during connectivity issues can reveal whether the problematic device is still advertising and what the signal environment looks like.

High Interference Environments

When wireless interference causes problems, scanning helps identify the source:

  • Many BLE devices: Crowded advertising environments strain radio resources.
  • Wi-Fi overlap: Wi-Fi channels 1, 6, and 11 overlap BLE channels.
  • Non-BLE interference: Some devices emit interference even without BLE.

If many BLE devices appear in scan results with weak signals, you may be in a congested environment. Switching Wi-Fi channels away from BLE frequencies can help for devices using Wi-Fi alongside BLE.

BLE Security Considerations

Understanding BLE Security Levels

BLE security has evolved significantly since its introduction:

  • Level 1: No security—no authentication or encryption.
  • Level 2: Unauthenticated pairing with encryption.
  • Level 3: Authenticated pairing with encryption.
  • Level 4: Authenticated LE Secure Connections with encryption.

Many legacy BLE devices still use Level 2 or Level 3, which have known vulnerabilities. Level 4 (LE Secure Connections) provides significantly improved security but requires both devices to support it.

Common BLE Vulnerabilities

BLE security issues that scanners can help identify:

  • Passive eavesdropping: Unencrypted connections allow data interception.
  • Man-in-the-middle attacks: Weak pairing allows attackers to insert themselves.
  • Device impersonation: Devices using predictable addresses can be spoofed.
  • Denial of service: Interference or rapid connection/disconnection attacks.

While the Bluetooth LE Scanner doesn't perform penetration testing, it helps identify devices that may warrant further security assessment.

Best Practices for BLE Security

To secure BLE deployments:

  • Use LE Secure Connections: Prefer devices supporting Level 4 security.
  • Keep firmware updated: Security patches address discovered vulnerabilities.
  • Disable unnecessary advertising: When not pairing, reduce discoverability.
  • Monitor device behavior: Unexpected advertising changes may indicate compromise.
  • Use bonded connections: Encrypted bonded connections resist eavesdropping.

BLE vs Other Wireless Technologies

BLE vs Classic Bluetooth

Classic Bluetooth and BLE serve different purposes despite sharing the Bluetooth name. Classic Bluetooth prioritizes continuous data streaming (audio, file transfer), while BLE prioritizes intermittent data and low power consumption.

Modern devices often support both, using Classic Bluetooth for audio streaming and BLE for device configuration and status updates. This dual-mode operation explains why some devices appear in BLE scans while primarily functioning via Classic Bluetooth.

BLE vs Wi-Fi

Wi-Fi provides higher throughput (up to gigabits per second) for data-intensive applications, while BLE provides lower throughput (up to 2 Mbps) for sensor data and control signals. Wi-Fi consumes more power and requires more complex protocols.

For IoT applications, BLE and Wi-Fi often work together—BLE handles device-to-phone communication for setup and monitoring, while Wi-Fi or other protocols handle internet connectivity for cloud communication.

BLE vs Other IoT Protocols

Other IoT protocols like Zigbee, Z-Wave, and Thread compete in similar spaces:

  • Zigbee: Mesh networking for home automation, similar power profile to BLE.
  • Z-Wave: Sub-GHz frequency for better range and penetration, proprietary.
  • Thread: IP-based mesh networking for smart home, often used with BLE for commissioning.

BLE's primary advantage is smartphone compatibility—nearly every smartphone supports BLE, while Zigbee and Z-Wave require dedicated hubs.
